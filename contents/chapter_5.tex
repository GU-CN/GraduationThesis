\chapter{Validation results}\label{chap:validation}

\section{Validation objectives and scope}

The validation evaluates the final unified $\pi_{0.5}$ system described in Chapter~\ref{chap:final-design}. Its principal purpose is to determine whether one jointly trained checkpoint can execute the three documented bimanual tasks on the physical dual-FR3 workcell and whether the completed prototype meets the six engineering specifications defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:design-spec}. The evaluation therefore separates three evidence families that have different meanings:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the offline normalized flow-matching loss used to select a checkpoint; and
  \item the separate 25-trial planning, detection, failure-detection, and recovery evaluations associated with ES1, ES3, ES5, and ES6; and
  \item the physical task outcomes measured over 75 trials for ES2 and used as the operational ES4 threshold checks.
\end{enumerate}
The first quantity measures agreement with held-out demonstration samples and is used for model selection. The second family evaluates defined intermediate engineering outcomes under separate 25-trial protocols. The third measures whether a complete physical trial reaches the task-specific terminal condition in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-task-definitions}. Because each terminal condition is geometrically restricted to a positional deviation below 20~mm, these same trials also provide the binary ES4 threshold check. Offline validation loss selects the checkpoint, while physical trials determine task success and ES4 threshold compliance at an accepted terminal state.

The physical ES2/ES4 evaluation covers the three task configurations used during final implementation: ring placement over a can, insertion of a two-pin plug into a two-hole socket, and memory-module removal from a motherboard followed by placement in a yellow tray. Each task was evaluated in 25 trials, giving 75 physical trials in total and 59 accepted terminal states. The ES1, ES3, ES5, and ES6 records each use 25 experiments under their own operational definitions. These populations are reported separately because their denominators and success events are not interchangeable.

\section{Validation design and engineering-specification mapping}

\subsection{Measurement rules and independent denominators}

Table~\ref{tab:ch5-es-protocol} maps every design-stage specification to its
final measurement rule. A rate is calculated from a binary outcome:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat p=\frac{x}{n},
  \label{eq:ch5-rate-estimator}
\end{equation}
where \(x\) is the number of successful outcomes and \(n\) is the number of
counted experiments for that specification. The project acceptance decision
uses the observed point estimate \(\widehat p\) and the threshold defined in
Table~\ref{tab:es}. Statistical intervals reported later show the uncertainty
associated with small samples.

\section{Checkpoint selection and deployment configuration}

\subsection{Selection criterion}

Chapter~\ref{chap:final-design} defines the checkpoint-selection procedure in Equation~\ref{eq:ch4-macro-flow-loss}. Validation was performed every 5,000 optimizer steps. For each validation point, the normalized flow-matching losses of the ring, socket, and RAM datasets were first calculated separately and then combined as the unweighted macro-average
\[
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{macro}}
=
\frac{
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ring}}
+
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{socket}}
+
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ram}}
}{3}.
\]
The checkpoint with the lowest recorded $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{macro}}$ was selected. The minimum occurred at 65,000 training steps, where the macro loss was 0.036552, and this checkpoint was used for physical deployment.

This selection preserves equal task importance. The ring dataset contains more frames than the other two datasets, but neither the training sampler nor the checkpoint-selection metric gives it greater task-level weight.

\subsection{Deployment hardware}

Training was performed on the RTX A6000 system documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:final-design}. Physical deployment used an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5070~Ti. The deployed policy used the same three image inputs, 16-dimensional bilateral state, absolute 16-dimensional action representation, and language-instruction interface as the training configuration. It predicted a 50-step action chunk and the executor consumed ten steps before requesting an updated prediction.

A complete task took approximately one minute. During deployment, the executor applied actions at 15~Hz and requested a new prediction after every ten executed actions.

\section{Physical trial protocol and success definition}

For each trial, the prepared scene corresponded to one of the three fixed language instructions in Table~\ref{tab:ch4-task-definitions}. The selected checkpoint was used for every task, with task identity provided through the language instruction.

The objects were reset before every trial and their initial positions were randomly perturbed around the nominal task arrangement. The permitted placement variation was approximately 5~cm for the ring and RAM scenes and approximately 3~cm for the socket scene. For each task, the 25 trials were conducted consecutively. Once autonomous execution started, no manual intervention or corrective assistance was permitted during a counted trial.

A trial was counted as successful only when its complete terminal condition was achieved:
\begin{itemize}
  \item for the ring task, the can was repositioned and the released ring remained over the can;
  \item for the socket task, the socket box was repositioned and the two-pin plug remained inserted in the two-hole socket; and
  \item for the RAM task, the memory module was fully removed from the motherboard and remained inside the yellow tray.
\end{itemize}
Failures were categorized by the first task stage that prevented the terminal condition. Partial completion was retained for failure analysis and counted as a failed trial.

The counted-trial record preserves the task, language instruction, checkpoint,
initial reset confirmation, autonomous start, terminal outcome, first blocking
stage, intervention status, and stop reason. These fields distinguish successful,
failed, assisted, and partially completed trials.

The ES1, ES3, ES5, and ES6 tests use their own 25-experiment records. They are
not added to the 75 ES2 denominator. Similarly, a recovery attempt recorded for
ES6 is a response to a detected failure and is not counted as a new ES2 task
success. This accounting prevents the same physical event from inflating more
than one success rate.

The randomized reset evaluates tolerance to moderate initial-position variation. The displacement limits were approximately 5~cm for the ring and RAM tasks and 3~cm for the socket task.

\subsection{Descriptive uncertainty of the observed rates}

The raw count is the primary result, but a confidence interval helps show how
much uncertainty remains with 25 task trials. For a binomial count \(x\) of
\(n\) trials, the two-sided 95\% Wilson interval is
\begin{equation}
  \frac{
  \widehat p+\frac{z^2}{2n}
  \ \pm\
  z\sqrt{
  \frac{\widehat p(1-\widehat p)}{n}
  +
  \frac{z^2}{4n^2}
  }
  }{
  1+\frac{z^2}{n}
  },
  \qquad z=1.96.
  \label{eq:ch5-wilson}
\end{equation}
Wilson intervals are used because they remain well behaved at boundary results
such as 25 successes in 25 trials.

\section{Task-specific results and failure analysis}

\subsection{Memory-module removal and tray placement}

The RAM task succeeded in all 25 counted trials, giving an observed success rate of 100\%. In the successful sequence, the left arm pulled and stabilized the motherboard while the right arm removed the memory module and transferred it to the yellow tray. No failure was recorded in the 25-trial set.

This result shows that the demonstration data, bilateral role allocation, camera observations, compliant grippers, and unified checkpoint worked reliably for the evaluated motherboard configuration.

\section{Cross-task interpretation}

The results demonstrate that the selected $\pi_{0.5}$ checkpoint can execute all three language-selected tasks. Performance is task dependent: the RAM task was fully successful in the observed trial set, the ring task was largely successful but sensitive to handle acquisition, and the socket task remained limited by precision insertion.

The observed failures occurred during grasping, placement, or insertion. Handle acquisition and insertion accuracy are therefore the main areas for improvement.

The current three scenes are visually distinct and each scene is paired with its expected instruction. Future tests can vary instruction wording and scene configuration to evaluate language sensitivity.

\subsection{Requirements-level interpretation}

At the project level, the rate-based specifications meet their stated
point-estimate thresholds, and the 59 accepted terminal states satisfy the ES4
pose-estimation-error threshold under the task-geometry rule. The configured scene and instruction generally form an
executable plan, required components are detected, the same unified checkpoint
completes all three task types, and most tested failure and recovery events are
handled successfully.

ES2 reaches 78.7\% overall, but socket insertion reaches only 52\%. Future
redesign should therefore focus on socket alignment and insertion accuracy.

\section{Validation limitations}

The validation has four main limitations.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Initial object positions were varied within approximately 5~cm for
        the ring and RAM tasks and 3~cm for the socket task, but individual
        coordinates were not logged.
  \item The visually distinct scenes were evaluated with their corresponding
        instructions, so instruction sensitivity was not tested separately.
  \item Each rate-based ES1, ES3, ES5, and ES6 result contains 25 observations.
        Their Wilson intervals therefore remain wide.
  \item ES4 was evaluated as a binary geometric threshold at accepted terminal
        states. No continuous millimetre-valued errors were recorded, and
        failed trials were not assigned a terminal pose error because they did
        not reach the state to which that terminal metric applies.
\end{enumerate}

The selected unified checkpoint executed all three tasks on the physical
dual-arm setup, with task-dependent reliability.

\section{Validation summary}

The selected checkpoint was evaluated in 75 physical trials. Objects were reset and varied before every trial, and the 25 trials for each task were conducted without manual intervention. The memory-module task achieved 25/25 successes, the ring task achieved 21/25, and the socket task achieved 13/25. The combined result was 59/75, or 78.7\%.

The additional engineering-specification tests produced ES1 planning success of 23/25 (92\%), ES3 component detection of 24/25 (96\%), ES5 failure detection of 23/25 (92\%), and ES6 recovery of 23/25 (92\%). These reported point estimates meet their stated thresholds, although each result is based on only 25 experiments. For ES4, the same physical evaluation comprised 25 trials per task and 75 in total. Its 59 accepted terminal states satisfied the pose-estimation-error limit because the task and fixture geometry excludes completion at an error of 20~mm or more. This records zero threshold violations among accepted states, not 59 continuous error measurements or an exact error of 0~mm.

Failure analysis shows that ring failures were dominated by unsuccessful handle acquisition, while socket failures were dominated by inaccurate alignment and insertion into the two-hole socket. The RAM and ring tasks exceeded the 70\% task-execution target, but the socket task did not. Precision plug insertion is therefore the principal remaining performance bottleneck.
